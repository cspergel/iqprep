Memory

Questions about holding a few things in mind for a moment and working with them: numbers forwards and backwards, pictures in order, letters and numbers sorted.

Short sequences are easy and long ones are hard for everyone, so every child reaches a point where they miss. Strategies like grouping and saying things quietly in your head make a real difference.

  • Number Echo
  • Picture Parade
  • Mix & Sort

Number Echo

In WISC-V terms: Digit Span. It practises: holding numbers in mind and repeating them forward, backward and in order.

Digit Span measures auditory working memory: holding and manipulating information you hear. Forward span reflects attention and storage; backward and sequencing add mental manipulation. It is one of the seven FSIQ subtests and half of the Working Memory Index.

How it plays

  1. Tap “I’m ready” and listen. The numbers won’t be shown!
  2. Then tap the numbers on the keypad.
  3. Tap Done when you have them all.

Strategies to show your child

  • Chunk it. Hear numbers in little groups of 2 or 3, like a phone number: 4-7 … 2-9 … 1.
  • Whisper practice. Silently repeat the numbers in your head while you listen, keep the list “fresh”.
  • Whiteboard brain. For backward, picture the numbers written on a board, then read them from right to left.
  • Smallest hunt. For smallest-to-biggest, find the smallest first, then the next. Watch for a number said twice!
  • Don’t start too fast. Wait until all the numbers are said before you start tapping.

Common slips

  • Starting to answer before the list ends and losing the tail.
  • Saying backward lists in forward order.
  • Dropping a repeated digit in sequencing.
  • Getting anxious after a miss, each new list is a fresh start.

Try at home

  • Read phone numbers or license plates aloud and have your child echo them back (then backward!).
  • “Grocery list” game: add one item each turn and recite the whole list.
  • Simon-style clapping or color-pattern games.
  • Ask your child to put three spoken numbers in order while walking or driving.

Picture Parade

In WISC-V terms: Picture Span. It practises: remembering a set of pictures in the order they were shown.

Picture Span measures visual working memory: briefly holding a set of pictures and their order in mind, then picking them out from look-alike distractors. It is one of the two Working Memory Index subtests (with Digit Span) and part of the Nonverbal Index. Getting all the right pictures in the wrong order earns partial credit, so the game reports “found them” and “in order” separately, order memory usually lags behind recognition in younger children. Like the real test, the game reuses a modest set of pictures, and on harder items the choices include pictures that were the answers last round, the child has to ignore what is familiar and remember what is new (picking one is counted as a “last-round” error).

How it plays

  1. Tap “I’m ready” and look closely. The pictures stay for just a few seconds.
  2. Then you’ll see lots of pictures. Tap the ones you saw, first one first.
  3. Little numbers show your order. Tap a picture again (or Undo) to take it back.
  4. Tap Done when you have them all.

Strategies to show your child

  • Name them silently. Say the names in your head while you look: “apple, car, star”. Names are easier to hold than pictures.
  • Silly story. Link them in a funny story: “The apple drove a car up to a star!” The story keeps the order.
  • Pairs. Group them in twos: “apple-car … star-bus”. Two little pairs are easier than four loose pictures.
  • Shelf in your head. Picture a shelf (or your bedroom) and put each picture in its own spot, left to right. Then “walk” along it.
  • Number tags. Give each picture its place number as you look: “one apple, two car, three star”.

Common slips

  • Tapping a picture that was in the last round, it feels familiar, but it was not in this one.
  • Looking at every picture at once instead of left-to-right, so the order is lost.
  • Picking a same-family foil (another fruit, another vehicle) instead of the exact picture.
  • Tapping the most memorable picture first rather than the first one shown.

Try at home

  • “What’s missing?”: show 3 to 6 toys in a row, cover them, mix them up, and ask your child to rebuild the row.
  • Lay out a few picture cards, flip them over, and take turns naming them in order.
  • In the car or store, look at three signs or items, then try to list them in the order you passed them.
  • Play Memory/Concentration with picture cards.

Mix & Sort

In WISC-V terms: Letter-Number Sequencing. It practises: hearing mixed letters and numbers, then saying numbers in order and letters in ABC order.

Letter-Number Sequencing measures auditory working memory with mental manipulation: holding a spoken list while re-ordering it by two different rules (number order and alphabetical order). It is a secondary Working Memory subtest and, with Digit Span, forms the Auditory Working Memory Index. Young children start with “qualifying-style” items (sort 2 to 3 numbers, or one number and one letter). The letters used are chosen so none sound alike or rhyme with a number.

How it plays

  1. Tap “I’m ready” and listen. Nothing is shown, just listen!
  2. Tap the numbers first, from smallest to biggest.
  3. Then tap the letters in ABC order.
  4. Use Undo if you tap the wrong one, then tap Done.

Strategies to show your child

  • Two pockets. While you listen, drop numbers in one “pocket” and letters in another. Then empty the number pocket first.
  • Sort as you go. Keep a sorted line in your head and slide each new one into its place as you hear it: “3… 1-3… 1-3-A…”.
  • Echo, then sort. Just remember the list exactly as you heard it, by whispering it in your head. Sort it only after the last one is said.
  • Number line. Picture a number line from 1 to 9. Light up each number you hear, then read the lit-up ones from left to right.
  • Alphabet song. For the letters, softly run through the ABC song and stop at each letter you heard.

Common slips

  • Saying the letters before the numbers.
  • Repeating the list in the order it was heard instead of sorting it.
  • Losing the last item or two because sorting started too early.
  • Mixing up the ABC order of letters that are far apart (e.g., R and M).

Try at home

  • Say three numbers and two letters in the car (“7, K, 2, A, 5”) and take turns sorting them aloud.
  • Play “Grocery sort”: list a few items and prices, then repeat the prices from cheapest to dearest.
  • Sing the alphabet song and practise finding where a letter lives (“Is M before or after R?”).
  • Card game: deal 4 number cards and 2 letter cards face up for 5 seconds, flip them, and say them back sorted.
