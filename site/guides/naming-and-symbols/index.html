Naming and symbol learning

Extra question types that are given less often: naming familiar things quickly, and learning a small picture language then reading it back.

These relate to reading and math fluency. A psychologist adds them when there is a specific question to answer, so your child may never see them.

  • Speedy Namer
  • Quick Count
  • Symbol Stories
  • Symbol Stories (Later)
  • Symbol Match

Speedy Namer

In WISC-V terms: Naming Speed Literacy. It practises: how quickly familiar things (colors, shapes, letters, numbers) can be named, linked to reading fluency.

Naming Speed Literacy measures rapid automatized naming (RAN): how quickly a child can see familiar things and pull their names from memory. It is a complementary measure (not part of the IQ): together with Naming Speed Quantity it forms the Naming Speed Index (NSI), which is strongly linked to reading fluency, slow naming is a known early marker of reading difficulties, independent of intelligence. As on the real test, the sheet depends on age: at 6, colour-object (“blue fish”) and size-colour-object (“big red cat”) pages; at 7 to 8, size-colour-object and letter-number pages; from 9, letters and numbers only. Each round is a short untimed practice page, then two short timed pages (trials) of one kind, the score uses both; when two kinds fit the age they take turns. As on the real test the colours are only red, blue and yellow (no green), so colour vision doesn’t get in the way. For the truest measure use “Say them, grown-up times”; tap mode is a solo practice version that adds a matching step (one tap per word). Score = pictures named correctly per minute. In tap mode the review also shows which part of a name slipped (size, colour or picture).

How it plays

  1. Pick how to play: say them out loud with a grown-up timing, or tap the answers yourself.
  2. Start at the top-left. Go along each row, left to right, like reading.
  3. First a little practice page. Then two short timed pages of the same kind, with a breather in between.
  4. Name (or tap) every one as fast as you can, for pictures say the size (big or little) if asked, the colour, then the picture.
  5. A slip? Keep going, don’t go back.

Strategies to show your child

  • Look one ahead. While your mouth says this one, your eyes are already on the next one.
  • Read it like a book. Left to right, then sweep down to the start of the next row. No skipping around.
  • Same order every time. Always say it the same way: big or little, then colour, then picture, “little yellow ball”. A fixed order stops mix-ups.
  • Find your beat. Say them to a steady rhythm, like a drum. A beat keeps you from stopping.
  • Let slips go. If a wrong name pops out, don’t stop to fix it, fixing costs more time than the slip.

Common slips

  • Stopping to self-correct after every slip.
  • Losing the place at the end of a row.
  • Leaving out a word (saying “red cat” when it was a little red cat), or swapping the order.
  • Adding extra words (“a red circle one”) instead of short names.

Try at home

  • Flash-naming races: flip colour, letter or number cards and say each as fast as possible; try to beat your time.
  • “Big or little?”, line up toys of two sizes and colours and name them in a row: “big blue car, little red ball…”.
  • Name the colours of cars driving past, as quick as you can.
  • Read rows of letters or numbers on licence plates, signs or a calendar out loud.

Quick Count

In WISC-V terms: Naming Speed Quantity. It practises: how quickly small quantities can be recognized without counting, linked to math fluency.

Naming Speed Quantity measures how quickly a child recognises small quantities and says the number, “subitizing” (seeing 1 to 4 items at a glance) plus rapid number naming. It is a complementary measure (not part of the IQ) that joins Naming Speed Literacy in the Naming Speed Index (NSI). Quick quantity naming is linked to early maths fluency and number sense. As on the real test, each round is a short practice page and then two short timed pages (trials); the score uses both. The truest version is spoken with a grown-up timing; tap mode is a solo practice variant. Score = boxes named correctly per minute.

How it plays

  1. Pick how to play: say the numbers out loud with a grown-up timing, or tap the number buttons yourself.
  2. Start at the top-left box and go along each row, left to right.
  3. Say (or tap) how many squares are in each box.
  4. First a little practice page, then two short timed pages, keep going until you reach the end!

Strategies to show your child

  • See, don’t count. Small groups of 1, 2 or 3 can be “just known” with one look. Trust your eyes!
  • Split it up. For bigger groups, see 2 and 2 (that’s 4) or 2 and 3 (that’s 5) instead of counting each one.
  • Shape spotting. Three squares often make a line or a triangle; four often make a box or a zig-zag. Learn the pictures numbers make.
  • Look one ahead. While you say this number, your eyes are already on the next box.
  • Keep the beat. Say the numbers in a steady rhythm. If one slips out wrong, keep going.

Common slips

  • Counting every square one by one (slow) instead of seeing groups.
  • Mixing up 4 and 5 when rushing.
  • Stopping to fix a slip instead of moving on.
  • Losing the place at the end of a row.

Try at home

  • Dice flash: roll one or two dice and shout the total as fast as you can (dot patterns build instant seeing).
  • Dot-card or domino flashes: show a card for one second, how many?
  • Ten-frame or egg-carton games: fill some spaces and say how many at a glance.
  • “How many?” on walks: birds on a wire, people at a bus stop, answer without pointing.

Symbol Stories

In WISC-V terms: Immediate Symbol Translation. It practises: learning picture-symbols for words and reading them back right away.

Immediate Symbol Translation measures visual-verbal associative learning: pairing new symbols with words and retrieving them right away, with the load growing as more symbols are learned. It is a complementary subtest; with Delayed and Recognition Symbol Translation it forms the Symbol Translation Index, which together with Naming Speed contributes to the Storage and Retrieval Index. Difficulty with this kind of paired learning can be an early signal worth discussing for reading (it is how letters and sight words are learned). Give the Delayed and Recognition games later in the same session to see how well the learning sticks.

How it plays

  1. First you meet new symbols. Tap each one to hear its word.
  2. Tap “Got it!” when you’re ready.
  3. Then read the row of symbols: tap a word tile for each symbol, left to right.
  4. Tap a word to put it in. Hold a word to hear it. Tap a word in a slot to take it out.
  5. Grown-ups can switch on “Grown-up listens” so you read it out loud instead.

Strategies to show your child

  • Picture-story. Make a tiny story for each symbol: “the wavy lines are waves of water”, “the fork is for eating”.
  • Say it twice. When you meet a symbol, say its word out loud (or in a whisper) two times while you look at it.
  • Trace it. Draw the symbol in the air or on your hand with your finger while you say its word.
  • Spot the clue. Find the part of the symbol that gives it away, the rays on the sun, the tail on the fish.
  • Look back. When new symbols arrive, quickly run through the old ones in your head too, so none get lost.

Common slips

  • Skipping the small function-word symbols (the, and, is, on).
  • Using the right words in the wrong order.
  • Rushing past the teach cards without saying each word.
  • Guessing from the first one or two symbols instead of reading the whole row.

Try at home

  • Invent a family “secret code”: draw simple symbols for 5 words and leave each other symbol notes.
  • Read rebus books or puzzles where pictures replace words.
  • Point out real-world symbols (road signs, washing labels, map keys) and talk about what they mean.
  • Learn a few signs in sign language or a few words in a new language and quiz each other later in the day.

Symbol Stories (Later)

In WISC-V terms: Delayed Symbol Translation. It practises: remembering the learned symbols after a delay, long-term recall.

Delayed Symbol Translation measures how well the symbol-word pairs learned earlier are retrieved after a delay (typically 20 to 30 minutes later in the session), without being shown again. It reflects long-term storage and retrieval of new associations. Together with Immediate and Recognition Symbol Translation it forms the complementary Symbol Translation Index. A big drop from the Immediate score suggests the learning was not consolidated, extra review and meaningful “stories” for each symbol help.

How it plays

  1. Look at the row of symbols. No new symbols this time!
  2. Remember what each one means.
  3. Tap a word tile for each symbol, left to right, then tap Done.
  4. Hold a word to hear it. Tap a word in a slot to take it out.

Strategies to show your child

  • Replay the story. Think back to the picture-story you made: “the wavy lines were water…”.
  • Trace to remember. Trace a tricky symbol in the air with your finger, sometimes your hand remembers what you said when you learned it.
  • Sure ones first. Read the symbols you’re sure of first, then work out the tricky ones from what’s left.
  • Skip the tricks. Some word tiles don’t belong. Only use a word if you can find its symbol in the row.
  • Sentence sense. Use the sentence shape: “the cat ___ the fish” needs a doing word, like “sees” or “eats”.

Common slips

  • Mixing up two symbols that were learned close together.
  • Remembering the nouns but forgetting the small words (the, and, is).
  • Putting two words in swapped places.
  • Giving up on a long row instead of decoding it symbol by symbol.

Try at home

  • At dinner, ask about something learned in the morning, practise remembering after a delay.
  • Make picture flashcards of new words and review them the next day.
  • Play “What did we see?” after a walk: recall signs, shops or animals in order.
  • Revisit your family “secret code” notes a day later and decode them together.

Symbol Match

In WISC-V terms: Recognition Symbol Translation. It practises: recognizing which word goes with a learned symbol, recognition memory.

Recognition Symbol Translation checks whether the symbol-word pairs learned earlier can be recognized when the answer is offered among choices. It is easier than free recall: a child who struggles on Delayed but does well here has stored the pairs but has trouble retrieving them on demand. It is the third part of the complementary Symbol Translation Index (with Immediate and Delayed Symbol Translation).

How it plays

  1. Look at the symbol.
  2. Listen to the four choices. Tap “Read the choices” to hear them again.
  3. Pick the word that goes with it.
  4. Tap Done.

Strategies to show your child

  • Picture-story. Remember the story: “the fork is for eating”, “the heart means likes”.
  • Try each word. Hold each choice up to the symbol: does “sun” fit the ring with rays? Yes!
  • Cross out. Rule out the words you know belong to other symbols.
  • Spot the clue. Look for the part of the symbol that looks like something, a tail, rays, a roof.
  • What kind of word?. Is it a thing, an action, or a tiny word? Plain marks like a dot, plus or equals are tiny words (“the”, “and”, “is”).

Common slips

  • Choosing a word that belongs to a similar-looking symbol.
  • Mixing up the small function-word symbols (the, and, is, on).
  • Answering too quickly without checking every choice.

Try at home

  • Play matching games with picture and word cards.
  • Point to signs and symbols when out and ask “what does that one mean?”.
  • Use a home “symbol menu” for chores or snacks and let your child read it.
