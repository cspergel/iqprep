Speed

Simple tasks done quickly and accurately against a clock: copying symbols from a key, spotting a symbol in a row, finding animals on a busy page.

Each item is easy; the challenge is a steady pace without careless slips. These are the questions where knowing the format in advance helps most, because the rules have to be understood before the clock starts.

  • Secret Code
  • Symbol Spy
  • Animal Hunt

Secret Code

In WISC-V terms: Coding. It practises: copying symbols from a key quickly and accurately, visual-motor speed.

Coding measures processing speed: how quickly and accurately a child can do a simple visual task, here, learning symbol pairs from a key and copying them. It draws on visual scanning, short-term associative learning, attention and fine-motor (pencil) speed. Coding and Symbol Search together form the Processing Speed Index (PSI), and Coding is one of the seven subtests behind the Full Scale IQ. Children under 8 get the shapes version (A); 8 and up get the number-symbol version (B). The score is boxes coded correctly in 2 minutes; mistakes are not subtracted but are tracked, including “rotation errors” (a mark drawn turned or mirrored). The default “Draw it” mode is closest to the real paper task: the child draws each mark and the app reads it (unclear marks count as mistakes). “Tap it” mode is easier and faster, the publisher withdrew its tap-based digital version because it inflated scores, so tap scores are scaled to 80% of the boxes done. For the closest practice, also try paper and pencil.

How it plays

  1. Look at the key at the top: each shape or number has a secret mark.
  2. Pip shows you a few, then you try six. Fix any that Pip says are wrong.
  3. Then the clock starts. Draw the mark in the glowing box with your finger (or tap it, in tap mode).
  4. Lift your finger and the next box lights up. Keep going until the time runs out!
  5. No erasing, just like with a pencil, “↩ Redo last box” fixes only the box you just did.

Strategies to show your child

  • Learn the busy pairs. After a few boxes, whisper the pairs you keep using (“star-plus, circle-dot”) so you don’t need the key every time.
  • Make a picture story. Link each pair with a tiny story: the circle has a dot like an eye, the star wears a plus like a sparkle. Stories stick!
  • Small and quick marks. The mark only needs to be clear, not beautiful. One quick, neat stroke beats a slow, perfect one.
  • Look one ahead. While your finger draws in this box, your eyes are already on the next one.
  • Peek, don’t stare. When you check the key, jump straight to the one you need instead of reading the whole key.

Common slips

  • Looking back at the key for every single box instead of learning the common pairs.
  • Turned marks (, for |, > for ^, ⊥ for T), called rotation errors.
  • Drawing slowly and perfectly: a quick, clear mark is all that’s needed.
  • Slowing right down to be perfect, or rushing so fast that mix-ups pile up.

Try at home

  • Make a secret-code alphabet together (☆ = A, ○ = B …) and write short messages to decode.
  • “Code race” on paper: give each number a doodle, then race to fill in a row of numbers with a sand timer.
  • Copy bead, LEGO or sticker patterns from a model as quickly as you can.
  • Timed mazes, dot-to-dots and tracing pages for pencil speed.

Symbol Spy

In WISC-V terms: Symbol Search. It practises: quickly deciding whether a target symbol appears in a group, visual scanning speed.

Symbol Search measures processing speed, how quickly a child can scan and compare simple visual information and make accurate same/different decisions. It also taps visual discrimination (telling look-alike, mirrored and rotated symbols apart), attention and decision speed. Together with Coding it makes up the Processing Speed Index (PSI). Children under 8 get version A (one target, three symbols); 8 and up get version B (two targets, five symbols). Because wrong answers are subtracted from right ones, impulsive guessing does not pay, steady, accurate scanning does. “Turned slips” (rotation errors) count taps on a flipped or turned copy of a target; “look-alike slips” (set errors) count taps on a symbol that matches a target except for one changed part.

How it plays

  1. First 3 practice rows, get each one right to start (a wrong try gets a tip, then try again).
  2. Look at the symbol (or two symbols) in the yellow box.
  3. Check the row. If you see the exact same symbol, tap it.
  4. If it isn’t there, tap NO.
  5. Only the middle row is live, the rows above and below are faded. After each tap the next row slides up. Keep going until the time runs out!

Strategies to show your child

  • Left to right, once. Check each symbol one time, from left to right, like reading. No zig-zagging back and forth.
  • Shape first, then direction. First ask “is it the same shape?” Only if yes, ask “does it point the same way?” Most symbols drop out at step one.
  • Find the odd part. Pick one special part of the target, a dot, a hook, a corner, and hunt just for that part.
  • Yes or no, then go. Say “yes” or “no” in your head the moment you know, tap, and move on. Staring longer rarely changes the answer.
  • NO is a real answer. About half the rows have no match. Tapping NO quickly is just as good as finding one.

Common slips

  • Tapping a flipped or turned copy of the target (rotation error) or a look-alike with one part changed (set error).
  • Checking the row again and again out of worry instead of deciding.
  • Forgetting that NO is an answer and searching too long when nothing matches.
  • Racing so fast that wrong answers cancel out the right ones.

Try at home

  • Spot-the-difference and Where’s-Waldo-style search books, against a sand timer.
  • “I spy” races: who can find the matching sign, letter or shape first on a walk or car ride.
  • Card games like Snap or Spot It/Dobble that reward quick matching.
  • Find the matching sock, puzzle piece or LEGO brick from a pile as fast as possible.

Animal Hunt

In WISC-V terms: Cancellation. It practises: scanning a busy page and marking every animal, selective attention and speed.

Cancellation measures processing speed with an emphasis on selective visual attention: scanning a busy page, deciding quickly what belongs to a category (animals) and ignoring look-alike or related distractors. It is a secondary Processing Speed subtest (it can stand in for Coding or Symbol Search but is not part of the PSI itself). The scattered and in-rows pages are compared to see whether an organized layout helps, children who search systematically usually do better on both. Score = animals marked minus non-animals marked; missed animals are counted separately. As on the real pages, the animals are spread evenly over the four quarters of the page, and every quarter has a few “related” foils (a bone, an egg, paw prints, yarn…), marks on those are shown separately, since they show a child grabbing what goes with animals rather than checking the category.

How it plays

  1. Look at the page full of little pictures.
  2. Tap every animal you see. It gets a mark.
  3. Tapped something that isn’t an animal? Tap it again to take the mark off.
  4. Keep hunting until the time runs out!

Strategies to show your child

  • Hunt like you read. Start at the top-left and go along each row, left to right, then down to the next row, even on the scattered page.
  • Four corners. Split the page into four parts in your head. Clear one part completely, then move to the next.
  • Spot eyes and legs. Animals have faces, eyes, legs, wings or fins. Let your eyes jump to anything with a face.
  • Ask “is it alive?”. Only animals count. A bone, an egg, paw prints or a ball of yarn is not an animal, even if it goes with one.
  • Quick and smooth. Tap and keep your eyes moving to the next picture, don’t stop to admire your marks.

Common slips

  • Searching randomly and re-checking the same areas instead of going row by row.
  • Marking things that go with animals (a bone, an egg) or toys that look like animals.
  • Slowing down on the scattered page instead of imposing your own rows.
  • Stopping too early before the time is up.

Try at home

  • Where’s-Waldo-style search books and “find all the ___” picture pages, against a timer.
  • Circle every letter “a” (or every number 3) on a page of a newspaper or magazine.
  • “I spy” races in the supermarket or on a walk: count every red car or every dog.
  • Hidden-picture puzzles and seek-and-find games.
