Using IQ Prep well before a test

The goal is a child who walks in knowing what the questions look like, not one who has been drilled.

Why familiarity matters

An ability test tries to measure how a child thinks. A question format they have never seen, a timer they did not expect, or a rule they misunderstood can cost points that have nothing to do with ability. Seeing each kind of question beforehand removes that noise, so the result says more about your child and less about surprise.

That is also the limit of what practice should do. IQ Prep does not try to raise a score, and nothing here can promise a higher one.

A calm plan

  • Short and often. Ten to fifteen minutes a few times a week, over two or three weeks, beats one long session.
  • Start with Learn with Pip. Tutor mode reads each question aloud, shows a worked example and explains every answer.
  • Then practise. Practice adapts to your child, aims for mostly wins and eases off after misses. Children under 9 get a gentler version.
  • Try one challenge near the end. Challenges use test-style order, timing and stopping rules, so your child feels how a session flows.
  • Keep the last few days light. A tired child does not show what they know.

What to avoid

  • Treating practice as pass or fail, or promising rewards for a score.
  • Hunting online for real test questions. They are protected, and a child who has seen them can make a result hard to interpret.
  • Pushing through tears. Stop, praise the effort, and come back another day.

What to tell your child

Something simple and true: they will do puzzles and answer questions with a friendly grown-up. Some questions are meant to be too hard for everyone, so it is fine to say “I don’t know” and move on. There is nothing to study and nothing to win.

The day before and the day of

  • A normal bedtime and a normal breakfast.
  • Glasses or hearing aids if your child uses them.
  • A bathroom stop before starting, and a snack for any break.
  • Your calm. Children read it.

About the numbers in IQ Prep

Reports show practice estimates on a familiar scale so you can see strengths and progress. They come from practice items, not a standardised test given by a trained examiner, so they are not IQ scores and should not be used to make decisions.

Every puzzle in IQ Prep is original and generated on the spot; none are real test questions, and practice results are estimates, not IQ scores. The aim is familiarity, so a test measures your child’s thinking rather than their surprise. For questions about a particular assessment, ask the psychologist who gives it.
