Words and ideas

Questions that ask a child to explain: how two things are alike, what a word means, what they know about the world, and what to do in everyday situations.

Most of these are answered out loud, so they test two things at once: knowing something, and putting it into words. In IQ Prep you can score them yourself as your child answers aloud (Grown-ups, then Settings), which is the closest match to how they are usually given.

Alike Game

In WISC-V terms: Similarities. It practises: verbal concept formation and abstract reasoning, explaining how two things are alike.

Similarities measures verbal concept formation and abstract reasoning: seeing the shared category or idea behind two words. With Vocabulary it makes up the Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI), and both count toward Full Scale IQ. On real tests answers are scored 2 (key category or essential shared idea, e.g. “both are fruit”), 1 (a concrete or partial likeness, e.g. “both have peels”) or 0 (a difference, true of only one, or too general). Here the pair is read aloud and your child says an answer in their own words first (as on the real test, where nothing is printed); the multiple choice that follows (“which is closest to what you said?”) teaches what a 2-point answer looks like. Turn on grown-up scoring to score the spoken answer yourself, the closest match to a real test. All pairs in IQ Prep are original practice items, not test items.

How it plays

  1. Listen to the two things.
  2. Say out loud how they are the same, in your own words.
  3. Then pick the answer that is closest to what you said.

Strategies to show your child

  • Find the family name. Ask: what GROUP do they both belong to? Fruit, animals, tools, feelings… A group name usually wins the most points.
  • What are they FOR?. If no group pops out, think about their job: what do people USE both of them for? (Both help you see in the dark.)
  • Where would you find them?. Picture where each one lives or is used, a kitchen, the sky, a farm. That place can lead you to the big idea.
  • Kinds of ___. Try the sentence “They are both kinds of ___.” If a word fits, that’s a strong answer.
  • Check BOTH. Test your answer on each thing: is it true for the first one? For the second one? If not, keep looking.

Common slips

  • Telling how the two things are different.
  • Describing only one of the two things.
  • Stopping at a small detail (both are red, both have legs) when a group name exists.
  • Giving an answer so general it fits anything (“they’re both things”).

Try at home

  • “How are these alike?” car game: take turns naming two things and finding the best group name.
  • Sort toys, laundry or groceries into groups and name each group.
  • Play “odd one out” with three foods or animals, then explain why.
  • Twenty Questions, asking “Is it an animal? A tool?” builds category thinking.

Word Wizard

In WISC-V terms: Vocabulary. It practises: word knowledge and the ability to explain what words mean.

Vocabulary measures word knowledge and the ability to explain meanings, a strong marker of language development and crystallized knowledge. With Similarities it makes up the Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI), and both count toward Full Scale IQ. Real tests start young children with a few picture-naming items, then ask for spoken definitions scored 2 (good synonym, main use, or category plus defining feature), 1 (vague, partial, or just an example) or 0 (wrong, a sound-alike, or only a gesture). Here the word is read aloud and your child says a definition first (in test mode the word is never printed, as on the real test); the multiple choice that follows shows what a 2-point definition sounds like. Grown-up scoring lets you score the spoken definition yourself. All words and definitions are original practice content.

How it plays

  1. Look at the picture or listen to the word.
  2. Say out loud what it is or what it means.
  3. Then pick the answer that is closest to what you said.

Strategies to show your child

  • Kind + special. Say what KIND of thing it is, then what makes it special: “A giraffe is an animal with a very long neck.”
  • What do you do with it?. For things, think about what it is USED for: “A ladder is something you climb to reach high places.”
  • Swap in a twin word. Find a word that means the same: “enormous” = “huge”. If the twin word fits, you’ve got it.
  • Put it in a sentence. Say a sentence with the word (“The glass is fragile”), then ask what the word is doing in that sentence.
  • Example is not enough. An example (“like a lion”) is a start. Ask: what is true about ALL of them? That’s the meaning.

Common slips

  • Acting the word out or pointing instead of using words.
  • Giving only an example (“like a lion”) instead of the meaning.
  • Using the word in its own definition (“fragile means it’s fragile”).
  • Confusing it with a word that sounds similar.

Try at home

  • Read aloud every day and pause on interesting words: “What do you think that means?”
  • Pick a “word of the day” and try to use it three times at dinner.
  • Play “define it” in the car: one person says a word, the other explains it in one sentence.
  • When your child gives an example, ask “What kind of thing is it? What makes it special?”

Know-It-All Quiz

In WISC-V terms: Information. It practises: breadth of general knowledge picked up from school, reading and the world around.

Information measures general knowledge picked up from everyday life, reading and school, part of crystallized intelligence. It is a secondary verbal subtest: it can substitute in the Verbal Comprehension area but is not part of the core VCI (which uses Similarities and Vocabulary). Items are scored 0 or 1, so there is no 2/1/0 rubric. Knowledge grows from curiosity and conversation more than drilling, the best preparation is a rich, talkative everyday life. All questions here are original practice content.

How it plays

  1. Listen to the question.
  2. Say your answer out loud. It’s OK to make your best guess!
  3. Then tap the answer that matches what you said.

Strategies to show your child

  • Picture it. Make a picture in your head: a spider, a calendar, a map. Count the legs or the months in your picture.
  • Where did I learn this?. Think: did I see this in a book, at school, on a trip, or on a show? Going back there can bring the answer.
  • Cross out the silly ones. Some answers are clearly wrong. Rule them out first, then pick from what’s left.
  • Say it in order. For days, months and numbers, say the list out loud: “…March, April…”
  • Use word clues. Parts of words give hints: “tri” means three, “octo” means eight, “geo” means Earth.

Common slips

  • Answering before hearing the whole question.
  • Freezing instead of giving a best guess.
  • Mixing up similar facts (spiders have 8 legs, insects have 6).

Try at home

  • Read non-fiction books together, animals, space, the human body, and chat about one fact each day.
  • Keep a family calendar where your child finds the day, date and month each morning.
  • Look at maps and globes: where do grandparents live? Where do penguins live?
  • Play a dinner-table quiz where everyone asks one question they learned today.

What Would You Do?

In WISC-V terms: Comprehension. It practises: practical reasoning and understanding of social rules and why they exist.

Comprehension measures practical reasoning and understanding of social rules and why they exist, common sense put into words. It is a secondary verbal subtest (it can stand in within the verbal area but is not part of the core VCI, which uses Similarities and Vocabulary). Real answers are scored 2 / 1 / 0, and some questions need two different general ideas for full credit (e.g. a safety reason plus a fairness or money reason). Grown-up scoring lets your child explain in their own words while you use the rubric. All situations here are original and gently worded.

How it plays

  1. Listen to the question.
  2. Say out loud what would really help, or why the rule exists.
  3. Then pick the answer closest to what you said.

Strategies to show your child

  • Safety first. Ask: what would keep everyone safe? The safest choice is often the best one.
  • Who does it help?. For “why” questions, think about WHO the rule protects or helps, you, other people, animals, the planet.
  • Find two reasons. Look for one reason about people (safety, fairness, feelings) and one about things (money, time, saving stuff).
  • Kind and fair. Imagine you were the other person. What would feel fair and kind to you?
  • Fix the whole problem. An answer that fixes only part (just “say sorry”) is OK, but one that fixes the whole thing is better.

Common slips

  • Giving only one reason when two are needed.
  • Saying “because it’s the rule” without explaining why.
  • Telling a personal story instead of a general reason.
  • Choosing a fast fix that only solves part of the problem.

Try at home

  • Ask “Why do you think we have that rule?” about everyday rules, seatbelts, lines, library due dates, and look for two reasons.
  • Play “What would you do if…?” at dinner with small, everyday problems.
  • Read stories and pause: “What should the character do now? Why?”
  • Talk through real choices your child makes: what happened, what else could they have done?

Every puzzle in IQ Prep is original and generated on the spot; none are real test questions, and practice results are estimates, not IQ scores. The aim is familiarity, so a test measures your child’s thinking rather than their surprise. For questions about a particular assessment, ask the psychologist who gives it.
